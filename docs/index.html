Three spacecraft, one solar wind

Three spacecraft now stream real-time solar wind from the Sun–Earth L1 point, about 1.5 million km sunward: NOAA's SOLAR-1 (operational since June 2026), NASA's IMAP, and the 28-year-old ACE. This page turns their measurements into the questions that matter: what is coming, how much warning we get, whether the instruments agree, and how much of Earth's response the wind actually explains.

Magnetic field and flagged events

Bz is the north–south part of the solar wind's magnetic field. When it points south (below zero) it links up with Earth's field and opens a door for energy; the longer and stronger it stays south, the bigger the response. Shaded bands are intervals the anomaly model flagged. Tap or click an event to zoom in.

How the anomaly model decides

An Isolation Forest looks at eight measurements for every minute of the past week: speed, density, field strength, Bz, ram pressure, and how fast speed, density and field strength changed over 10 minutes. It repeatedly splits the data at random; minutes that get isolated in very few splits are unlike the rest, and the most isolated 1% are flagged. For each event the cards list the measurements that sat furthest from the week's median, in units of their usual spread. The model always flags its quota, even in a calm week, so a flag alone only means unusual for this week. An event is called significant only if it also crosses a physical threshold: a speed jump of 50 km/s or more, Bz at or below −10 nT, or pressure or field strength 2.5 times the week's normal. It matters for Earth when Bz is also strongly south.

How a southward field lets energy in

A simplified 3D model of Earth's magnetic shield. The magnetopause (the shield's edge) is sized from the solar wind's pressure with the Shue et al. (1998) model. With Bz north, solar wind particles flow around the shield. Turn Bz south and field lines reconnect at the front: particles are funnelled down to the polar regions, where they light the aurora. Does the wind at L1 predict Earth's response?

Coupling estimates how much solar wind energy can enter Earth's magnetic field. Kp measures how disturbed that field actually becomes (0 calm, 5 or more a storm). Each L1 reading is first shifted forward by its own travel time to Earth, so cause and effect are compared at the moment they meet.

Reading ρ and R²

Spearman's ρ (rho) asks one question: when coupling is higher than usual, is Kp also higher than usual? It ranks both lists and compares the rankings, so it doesn't assume a straight-line relationship. +1 means they always rise together, 0 means no relationship, −1 means one rises as the other falls. Above about 0.7 is strong for messy natural data.

R² is the share of Kp's ups and downs a model reproduces. 0.65 means 65% is explained and 35% comes from something the model doesn't see. Adding factors one at a time and watching R² grow shows what each one contributes.

Do the three spacecraft agree?

Two separate questions. Calibration: does a spacecraft read steadily high or low compared with the others? That's normal for real-time data from different instruments and can be corrected. Tracking: once that steady offset is removed, does it rise and fall with the others? Readings are first lined up in time using each spacecraft's position, then compared in 5-minute steps: in step means speed within 4%, density within 20% and Bz within 2 nT of the other spacecraft.

  • Two agree, one differs: trust the pair. The odd one is likely an instrument issue or sits in a different patch of wind.

  • One flips Bz sign while the others hold steady: the most important disagreement, because the sign of Bz decides whether energy gets in.

L1 is not Earth
